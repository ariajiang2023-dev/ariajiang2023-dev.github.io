Hi! I'm **Xinyi (Aria) Jiang**, an M.S. student in Economics at the
University of Wisconsin–Madison, applying to PhD programs for Fall 2027.

My research sits at the intersection of **econometrics, measurement,
and AI**. I am interested in **methods for reliable estimation,
inference, and measurement when labeled data are scarce, dependent, or
generated by changing processes**, and in using them to design and
evaluate AI tools that people and public institutions rely on,
especially in education and for those with the fewest resources.

The questions I am most drawn to:

- **How can we draw reliable conclusions when labels are scarce?**
  My replication work on semi-supervised learning led me to ask this
  question for panel and time-series data, where labels are costly and
  the underlying process can change. I am now working on a project that
  explores semi-supervised methods for this setting.
- **When should an AI tool use context, and when should it ask for
  clarification?** My current research runs controlled experiments to
  test whether telling a language model the user's professional field
  changes how it interprets ambiguous terms. I'd like to use these
  findings to design and test tools that help people navigate public
  services, including when the tool should ask a follow-up question
  before offering guidance.
- **How do we know whether an AI answer is actually better?** Leading
  the human scoring in this project showed me that evaluating AI
  responses is itself a measurement problem: raters disagree, and
  test questions differ in difficulty. I am interested in using
  measurement models such as item response theory to evaluate AI tools
  more reliably.
- **How does access to different AI tools shape educational
  opportunity?** Do free and paid AI tools differ in the quality of
  guidance they give students, for example on financial aid, and does
  better guidance change the choices students make? Because students
  who pay for premium AI differ in many ways from those who don't,
  separating the effect of the tool from who uses it is a core
  empirical challenge.
